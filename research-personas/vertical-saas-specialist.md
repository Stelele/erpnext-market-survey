# Vertical SaaS Specialist

## Who They Are

The founder who deliberately chooses industry-specific niches — dentists, lawyers, HVAC contractors, gyms, manufacturers, wedding planners — over horizontal tools that serve everyone. They understand that vertical SaaS has less competition, higher willingness to pay, and stronger word-of-mouth in tight-knit industries. They are not generalists who wandered into a niche; they chose the niche because the alternative — competing in a horizontal market — is suicide for a bootstrapped company.

## What Drives Them

Driven by the insight that serving a specific vertical deeply is more defensible than serving many verticals shallowly. They research their target industry extensively, learning the jargon, workflows, regulatory environment, and business model before writing a line of code. They build domain expertise as a moat. Their competitive set is not other SaaS companies — it's the spreadsheets, legacy software, and manual processes their customers use today.

## Discovery Triggers

- They notice a vertical has legacy software that hasn't been updated in a decade
- They find a vertical where the dominant solution is "use Excel"
- They hear the same business problem described differently by people in the same industry (signals common pain)
- They discover a vertical where there's one or two dominant players but they're overpriced or outdated
- They find a vertical SaaS company that recently raised funding — funding in a vertical is a signal of market size
- They notice that consultants and agencies serving a vertical keep recommending the same workarounds

## Validation Threshold

They validate vertical demand by talking to 10-20 people in the target industry before building anything. They look for trigger events — new hires, regulatory changes, new software adoption — that force vertical customers to reevaluate their tools. They price based on the value the vertical places on solving the problem, not on what horizontal competitors charge.

## Red Flags

- Choosing a vertical based on personal interest rather than market readiness
- Entering a vertical where there's already a dominant, well-funded incumbent
- Underestimating the regulatory complexity of serving certain verticals
- Conflating "nobody has built for this vertical" with "there's demand here" — sometimes there's no product because there's no market
- Building for a vertical where the average customer is too small to afford SaaS pricing
- Ignoring the sales cycle in regulated verticals — lawyers and doctors take months to decide

## ERPNext Ecosystem Lens

ERPNext already positions itself as a vertical-ready platform — it has industry-specific modules for manufacturing, healthcare, education, and retail. The Vertical SaaS Specialist in this ecosystem sees ERPNext as the foundation and builds the layer above it that makes ERPNext genuinely usable for specific verticals. ERPNext's "good enough for everyone, great for no one" positioning is the opportunity.

**ERPNext opportunity signals:**
- Forum posts from companies in specific verticals saying "ERPNext doesn't really work for our industry out of the box"
- Implementation partners who specialize in specific verticals and have built internal IP
- ERPNext community requests for "industry-specific templates" or "vertical-specific setups"
- Companies migrating to ERPNext from vertical-specific legacy software (SAP for manufacturing, Dentrix for dental)
- ERPNext users in specific verticals who have built significant customizations that could be templatized

**Pain points this persona feels:**
- ERPNext's vertical modules are incomplete — manufacturing has gaps, healthcare has compliance gaps
- Vertical-specific reporting and compliance requirements aren't built into ERPNext
- ERPNext's one-size-fits-all doctype system doesn't match vertical-specific data models
- Industry-specific integrations (POS for retail, lab equipment for healthcare) are missing
- ERPNext implementation for verticals requires significant customization that becomes technical debt
